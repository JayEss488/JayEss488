Newton’s laws of motion in missile propulsion



D espite being an ever-growing field of innovation and research, and a marvel of modern innovation, fundamentally, the concepts that underpin how a missile works and how they are improved over time are Newton’s laws of motion, first published in his principia [2] all the way back in 1687! This will be put into context through the propulsion system of missiles. First, let's have a look at Newton's three laws of motion in their purest form.


Newton's three laws


Law 1

This is the law that states that if there is no resultant force on a closed system then it will either stay at rest or continue at the same, constant velocity.


Law 2

This is the law that the net force on a closed system is directly proportional to the resultant acceleration, summarised by the equation F = ma where m is the mass.


Law 3

This law states that 'every action has an equal and opposite reaction'.


General overview of propulsion




Fun Fact

Temperatures at the nozzle typically reach 2000-3500°C! [4]



As illustrated from the diagram above, the missile propulsion system (rocket engine) works in in three steps:


The fuel chamber

This contains either solid propellant or liquid propellant, but is sometimes even a hybrid of the two types [4].


Mixing of fuel with oxidiser

The fuel is mixed with an oxidiser. This causes a chemical reaction, producing a gaseous propellant at high pressure, which is subsequently passed through an igniter between the fuel chamber and exhaust nozzle. The oxidiser means that the missile can operate outside of the atmosphere (in order to reduce drag), so it can continue burning in low-oxygen conditions and even the vacuum of space.


Ignition

The gaseous propellant ignites, sending gas particles flying out of the exhaust nozzle at extreme speeds. This pushes the rocket forward, as explained below.


A closer look: Momentum and forces




Newton's laws



We all know that rockets are pushed forward by the exhaust, but how can this be explained at an atomic level, and how are Newton’s laws relevant? Let us consider a singular gas particle and the rocket, as shown in the diagram above. The chemical energy released during the ignition of the propellant will cause gas particles to rapidly spread out, directed outwards by the nozzle. Considering the system just after the moment where the gas is ignited, a force is exerted on the particle which causes it to accelerate and have a large velocity (Newton’s second law). However, Newton’s third law states that ‘every action has an equal and opposite reaction’, so the missile must experience an equal force in the opposite direction,Fp .

If we increase this to the real-life scale of, for example, several kilogrammes of liquid fuel burnt per second, there will be large orders of magnitude of atoms, each producing a force Fp, so the resultant force on the missile will be the sum of all these and will be large. Obviously, using higher density liquid fuels will mean more acceleration per unit volume of fuel consumed, and therefore a larger range which is why high-density liquid fuels such as RJ6 are popular [5].


Momentum explanation (derived from Newton’s 3rd law)



We can also use the law of conservation of momentum to explain missile propulsion. Consider the closed system including the missile and all its fuel with constant initial momentum. For every gas particle ejected from the ignited propellant at a speed vp m/s, it will carry a momentum of vpmp kgm/s in the positive x-direction (indicated on diagram). In order to conserve momentum, the resulting momentum change of the rocket is vmmm kgm/s in the negative x direction such that vpmp + vmmm=0 . Therefore, the velocity of the missile will increase for ejected gases and, again, on large scales this will be measurable.


Launching Off




In order for a missile to successfully launch, and stay in the air, the vertical component of thrust produced by the propulsion system must be greater than the weight of the missile, or else it will fail to launch/will accelerate downwards. This is clear by looking at Newton’s first and second laws of motion, and the diagram above illustrates the need to consider vector force components, and how we can predict whether a launch will be successful, and how fast the missile will accelerate from a known thrust Fm and launch angle for non-vertical launches.

It also follows that changing the angle of the exhaust nozzle relative to the body of the missile mid-transit (hence the direction of Fm) will change the direction of acceleration of the missile so we can control its motion.


For example, the AIM-9 Sidewinder is a tracking missile which uses a sensor to detect infrared radiation (heat) from enemy aircraft, and, using an automatic guidance system, makes alterations on its flight path to track and destroy them – this process is called thrust vectoring [8].
